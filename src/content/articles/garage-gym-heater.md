This guide is based on the US Department of Energy's space heater guidance and the US Consumer Product Safety Commission's carbon monoxide warnings, listed at the end. We do not link to specific heaters yet. Anything hardwired or gas-fired is a job for a licensed electrician or gas fitter, and your local code decides what is allowed in an attached garage.

## The short answer

| Garage | Good choice | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One-car or small, insulated | 1,500 W plug-in electric heater | Extension cords, leaving it on unattended |
| Two-car, some insulation | Hardwired 240 V electric heater (about 4,000 to 7,500 W) | Needs a dedicated circuit from an electrician |
| Used most of the year | Ductless mini-split heat pump | Higher upfront cost |
| Any garage gym | Insulate the door and seal gaps first | Heating a garage with a bare metal door wastes most of the heat |
| Never while training | Unvented propane or kerosene heaters | Carbon monoxide |

## Why garage gyms are hard to heat

A garage is usually the least insulated room attached to a house. The big door is often bare metal, the slab floor stays cold, and gaps around the door let warm air out. That is why a heater that warms a bedroom barely touches a garage.

Before you buy a bigger heater, fix the cheap things:

1. **Insulate the garage door.** Foam panel kits fit most sectional doors.
2. **Replace the bottom seal and weatherstripping.**
3. **Put down rubber flooring.** It makes the floor feel warmer underfoot and protects the slab. See our guide to [garage gym ideas](/garage-gym-ideas/) for layout tips.
4. **Insulate the walls and ceiling** if they are open studs.

## The three types of heater

### Electric (plug-in or hardwired)

Electric heaters make no fumes, which matters when you are breathing hard. The Department of Energy notes they can cost more to run than combustion heaters, and they still carry burn and fire risks.

- **Plug-in 120 V, up to 1,500 W.** About **5,100 BTU per hour** (1 watt is 3.412 BTU per hour). Fine for a small, insulated space or to warm the corner you train in.
- **Hardwired 240 V, about 4,000 to 7,500 W.** A 5,000 W unit puts out about **17,000 BTU per hour**. Mounted high on a wall or ceiling, out of the way of barbells. Needs a dedicated circuit.
- **Infrared or radiant panels** warm you and the equipment directly rather than the air, which helps in a drafty garage.

What to look for, per the Department of Energy: a safety certification label such as UL, a **thermostat**, and a **tip-over switch** on anything that sits on the floor.

### Combustion (propane, natural gas, kerosene)

This is where garage gyms go wrong. The Department of Energy is direct: **unvented combustion heaters are not recommended for use inside your home because of the risk of carbon monoxide poisoning.** Vented units that pull combustion air from outside and exhaust it outside are much safer, and the department recommends a yearly inspection for them.

Carbon monoxide has no smell or color. The CPSC's generator warnings make the same point for any fuel-burning engine near a house. A hard workout raises your breathing rate, so you take in more of whatever is in the air.

If you go with gas, have a licensed installer fit a **vented** unit to code, and put a **carbon monoxide alarm** in the garage either way.

### Ductless mini-split heat pump

A mini-split heats and cools, so it also makes a summer garage gym bearable. It costs more upfront and needs an installer, but it is the most comfortable year-round option if you train in the garage most days.

## Sizing: how big a heater?

Honest answer: it depends on insulation, ceiling height and how cold your winters get, and we cannot give one number that fits every garage. Use the manufacturer's coverage rating (square feet at a given temperature rise) and size up if your garage is poorly insulated.

The Department of Energy also warns against buying an oversized heater, since it wastes energy and cycles on and off.

A rough guide to output:

| Heater | Output |
| --- | --- |
| 1,500 W plug-in | about 5,100 BTU/hr |
| 4,000 W hardwired | about 13,600 BTU/hr |
| 5,000 W hardwired | about 17,000 BTU/hr |
| 7,500 W hardwired | about 25,600 BTU/hr |

## Safety checklist

- Plug portable heaters **straight into the wall**. If you must use a cord, use the shortest heavy-duty cord you can, **14-gauge or larger**, per the Department of Energy.
- Keep heaters well away from chalk bags, foam mats, towels and anything flammable.
- Mount hardwired heaters where a swinging barbell or a falling plate cannot reach them.
- Never leave a portable heater running while you are out of the garage.
- Fit a **carbon monoxide alarm** in any garage with a fuel-burning appliance or a car.
- Never run a generator or a car in the garage to keep it warm.

## Training in a cold garage

Warm the space 20 to 30 minutes before you start, then turn the heater down once you are moving. Keep your warm-up longer than usual and layer clothes you can peel off. Cold barbells and dumbbells are hard on the hands, so a heater aimed at the rack area helps more than heating the whole room.
