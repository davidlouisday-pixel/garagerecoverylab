All prices here are regular list prices for the sauna itself. Budget extra for wiring, a base, shipping and possibly a heater.

## Barrel vs cabin vs cube

**Barrel saunas** are the most common outdoor choice. The round shape means less air to heat for the same bench space, and rain and snow slide off. Most ship as kits that bolt together with steel or aluminum bands. The trade-off is headroom: you only stand up straight in the middle.

**Cabin saunas** have straight walls and a pitched or flat roof. They feel more like a small room, give more headroom, and fit more people, but have more air to heat, so they often need a larger heater.

**Cube saunas** are a modern take on the cabin, often with large glass panels. Glass loses heat much faster than insulated wood, so a glass-heavy design needs more heater power. Our [sauna heater size calculator](/sauna-heater-size-calculator/) shows how much.

## What to check before you buy

1. **Heater and power.** Is a heater included, what size is it, and what circuit does it need? A 6 kW heater on 240V draws 25 amps. Plan the electrician before the sauna arrives.
2. **Wood.** Most models here offer cedar. Spruce, fir and pine are also used, sometimes stained.
3. **Interior size, not just seat count.** Seat counts vary between listings for the same model. The Dundalk Serenity's title says 3 to 4 people while its description says five. Compare interior dimensions instead.
4. **Base.** You need a level, solid base such as a concrete pad, pavers or a deck rated for the weight. The SaunaLife G4 kit ships at 1,650 pounds.
5. **Assembly.** Many barrels are DIY kits that take two people a day or two. Check what tools are needed and whether professional assembly is available in your area.
6. **Warranty.** Read the actual terms. Retailer pages are not always clear, and one page we checked listed two different warranty lengths for the same sauna.
7. **Permits.** Check with your town about permits for the structure and the electrical work.

## Traditional or infrared outdoors?

Most outdoor saunas use traditional heaters, which run in the 150 to 195°F range Cleveland Clinic describes for traditional saunas and let you pour water on the stones. Some infrared units are rated for outdoor use, but infrared runs much cooler. Our [infrared vs traditional sauna](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/) guide compares the two, and [how hot should a sauna be](/how-hot-should-a-sauna-be/) covers the temperatures used in the research.
