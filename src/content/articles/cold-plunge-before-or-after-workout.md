This guide is based on the studies listed at the end. Cole trains seven days a week and uses a sauna after training, but he **does not cold plunge**, so everything here about the cold comes from research.

## The short answer

| Your main goal | When to plunge |
| --- | --- |
| Muscle size (hypertrophy) | Not right after lifting; rest days or several hours later |
| Strength | Same caution; results are mixed, so keep it away from lifting |
| Endurance (running, cycling) | After is fine; it does not appear to hurt aerobic fitness |
| Fast recovery between games or sessions | After; it reduces soreness |
| Waking up | Before is fine, then warm up properly |

## The evidence against plunging right after lifting

### Roberts et al., 2015

In a study in the *Journal of Physiology*, **21 active men** strength-trained **twice a week for 12 weeks**. After every session, half did **10 minutes of cold water immersion** and half did active recovery (easy cycling). **Strength and muscle mass increased more in the active recovery group.** Muscle fiber size and the number of nuclei per fiber grew in the active recovery group but not the cold water group. A second experiment in the same paper found weaker muscle-building signals after cold water.

### Fyfe et al., 2019

This study in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* found post-exercise cold water immersion **reduced muscle fiber growth** with whole-body resistance training, though **strength gains were not affected**. The authors' advice was direct: cold water immersion after exercise **should be avoided if muscle growth is the goal**.

### Malta et al., 2021

A systematic review and meta-analysis concluded that regular cold water immersion alongside exercise programs **has a harmful effect on resistance training adaptations** but **does not appear to affect aerobic exercise performance**.

Why it might happen: the leading explanation is that cold dampens the inflammation and blood flow signals that help muscles adapt after training. That is a mechanism researchers propose, not something every study has shown directly.

## The evidence for plunging after training

Cold water still has a real use. An **updated 2022 meta-analysis** of cold water immersion for muscle soreness found:

- Cold water immersion was **better than no treatment** for soreness.
- **Water temperature and protocol did not change** that result.
- **Short and medium immersion times** worked; longer immersions did not show the delayed benefit.
- For the quickest effect, the best results were after **endurance** exercise. For the delayed effect, it helped after both endurance and resistance exercise.

So if you have a game tomorrow, a hard run block, or two sessions in one day, a plunge after training can make sense. That is the trade-off: faster recovery now, possibly smaller gains later if you lift.

## Plunging before a workout

A short plunge before training can make you feel alert. But cold, stiff muscles are not ideal for heavy lifting or sprinting. If you plunge before training:

- Keep it short.
- **Warm up thoroughly** afterwards before any heavy sets.
- Never plunge alone, and get in slowly; sudden cold triggers a gasp reflex and a jump in heart rate.

## A simple plan

- **Lifting for size:** plunge on rest days, or at least several hours after lifting. On lifting days, finish with a sauna instead; see [sauna before or after a workout](/sauna-before-or-after-workout/).
- **Mixed training:** plunge after cardio or conditioning sessions, not after heavy lifting.
- **In season or competing:** plunge after games and hard sessions when recovery speed matters most.

## Cole's take

Cole's post-workout routine is the gym sauna, about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, four or more times a week. He has not tried cold plunging, so he cannot tell you how it feels. Since he trains for strength and size, the research above is the reason he would keep any cold plunge away from his lifting sessions if he started.
