This guide is based on published studies and reviews, listed at the end. Cole uses a traditional rock-heater sauna at his gym, not an infrared one, so there is no first-hand infrared section here.

## The short answer

- **Best evidence for infrared:** small trials in people with heart failure and other cardiovascular conditions, chronic pain, chronic fatigue and a few other conditions, mostly with positive results.
- **Some evidence:** small post-workout recovery trials.
- **Not shown for infrared:** the large long-term findings on heart disease, dementia and stroke. Those came from traditional saunas.
- **Safety:** side effects reported in studies were mostly mild, such as heat discomfort.

## How infrared differs

A traditional sauna heats the air, usually to 150 to 195°F. An infrared sauna uses panels that warm your body more directly, so the room stays cooler, about **110 to 135°F** according to Cleveland Clinic. That gentler air temperature is one reason people who find traditional saunas too intense often prefer infrared. Our [infrared vs traditional sauna](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/) guide compares the two on wiring, heat-up and running cost.

## What the research shows

### The big systematic review

The most complete summary is a 2018 systematic review by Hussain and Cohen. It found **40 clinical studies with 3,855 participants** on regular dry sauna use, both infrared and traditional. Only **13 were randomized controlled trials**, and most studies were small, often under 40 people.

Conditions where studies reported benefits included:

- **Heart failure and other cardiovascular disease**, where most studies used far-infrared saunas
- **Peripheral artery disease**
- **Chronic pain** and **chronic tension headache**
- **Chronic fatigue syndrome** and **fibromyalgia**
- **Rheumatoid arthritis** and **ankylosing spondylitis**
- **COPD** and **allergic rhinitis**

The authors concluded that regular dry sauna bathing "has potential health benefits", and that more, higher-quality data is needed. They noted that many studies were small and only a few were randomized. Many of the far-infrared heart studies were done in hospital patients, so the results may not carry over to healthy people using a home unit.

### Recovery after exercise

Two small trials from Finland looked at infrared saunas after training:

- **Mero and colleagues, 2015:** **10 active men** sat in a far-infrared sauna for **30 minutes at about 95 to 122°F** after strength or endurance sessions. After the endurance session, jump height at 30 minutes of recovery was slightly better than without the sauna. After strength training, there was no clear difference. The project was partly funded by the sauna manufacturer Harvia.
- **Ahokas and colleagues, 2023:** a post-exercise infrared session at about 109 to 122°F improved recovery of neuromuscular performance and reduced soreness after resistance training. We cover this in [sauna for muscle recovery](/sauna-for-muscle-recovery/).

Mero's team also described the infrared session as a very light load on the body. Heart rate after infrared averaged **71 beats per minute**, against **92** after a traditional sauna session in the same study.

### What infrared research does not show

The famous Finnish results, such as **63% lower risk of sudden cardiac death** and **66% lower risk of dementia** with 4 to 7 sessions a week, come from traditional saunas averaging about 174°F. We cover those in [how hot should a sauna be](/how-hot-should-a-sauna-be/). No comparable long-term study has followed infrared users for decades. Infrared may well share some of those effects, but that has not been shown.

## Are infrared saunas safe?

The 2018 review found side effects were mostly mild, such as discomfort with the heat. In one chronic fatigue study, so many participants found the heat hard to tolerate that the researchers changed the protocol. The only adverse health outcome the review highlights is from one small study of **10 men**, which found **disrupted sperm production** that recovered after they stopped using the sauna. If you are trying to conceive, that is worth knowing.

The usual sauna cautions apply to infrared too. Cleveland Clinic advises avoiding saunas after a recent heart attack or stroke and after alcohol, and checking with a doctor first if you are pregnant, over 65, have heart or blood pressure problems, epilepsy, or take regular medication. The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review adds fever or acute infection, unstable angina, severe aortic stenosis and decompensated heart failure to the list of reasons to avoid sauna use.

## Who infrared suits

Infrared is a good fit if you find traditional sauna heat too intense, want a plug-in install, or want lower running costs. If your main reason for buying a sauna is the long-term heart research, a traditional sauna is the closer match to what was studied. Our [best home sauna](/best-home-sauna/) guide covers both types.
