This guide is based on research, not hands-on testing: we compared manufacturer specifications, published cost data and medical guidance. Sources are listed at the end.

## The short version

| | Infrared sauna | Traditional sauna |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air temperature | About 110 to 150°F | About 150 to 195°F |
| Heat-up time | 10 to 15 minutes | 30 to 45 minutes |
| Typical power (2-person) | 1.5 to 1.7 kW | About 4.5 kW |
| Electrical | Often 120V plug-in | Dedicated 240V circuit |
| Steam | No, dry heat only | Yes, water on hot stones |
| Typical price | About $1,500 to $7,000 | Kits from a few thousand; built-in rooms $10,000+ |
| Est. energy per session* | $0.20 to $0.30 | $0.50 to $0.70 |

*Our estimate: a 1.6 kW infrared unit running 45 minutes uses about 1.2 kWh; a 4.5 kW traditional heater preheating for 35 minutes and cycling through a 30-minute session uses about 3 to 4 kWh. Priced at the U.S. average residential rate of about 18 cents per kWh. Your rate may be higher or lower.

## How each one heats you

A **traditional (Finnish) sauna** uses an electric or wood stove to heat the air and a pile of stones. You sit in air that is 150°F or hotter, and ladling water onto the stones creates bursts of steam that make it feel even hotter. The air itself is dry, around 10% humidity, until you add water.

An **infrared sauna** uses carbon or ceramic panels that emit infrared light. The panels warm your body more directly, so the air only needs to reach about 110 to 150°F. There are no stones and no steam.

That difference drives everything else on this page: temperature, install, running cost and how the session feels.

## Install and electrical: the deciding factor for many buyers

This is where people get surprised.

- **Infrared:** many one- and two-person units plug into a standard 120V, 15 amp outlet. Larger three- and four-person cabins often need a dedicated 20 amp or 240V circuit. Check the spec sheet before you order.
- **Traditional:** a two-person room with a 4.5 kW heater typically needs a dedicated 240V circuit (about 19 amps). Bigger rooms use 6 to 9 kW heaters. Plan on hiring a licensed electrician, and budget for a panel upgrade if your panel is already full.

If you rent, have an older electrical panel, or want the sauna in a spare bedroom, infrared is usually the simpler path.

## The experience

People who grew up with Finnish saunas often find infrared underwhelming: the air is cooler and there is no steam. People who dislike intense heat often find infrared easier to stay in for 20 to 30 minutes.

Traditional saunas also suit groups and social use better, since the whole room is hot. Infrared panels heat what is in front of them, so seat position matters.

## Health claims: be skeptical of "better"

Both types raise your core temperature and make you sweat. Cleveland Clinic notes that some studies suggest regular sauna use may support heart health, but more research is needed, especially for infrared, and many pain and sleep studies are small. Claims that infrared is healthier than traditional are not settled.

Talk to your doctor first if you are pregnant, trying to conceive, have a heart condition or multiple sclerosis, or are unwell. Start with short sessions of 5 to 10 minutes and drink water.

## Which should you buy?

**Choose infrared if:**

- You want to plug it into an existing outlet
- You care most about running cost and quick heat-up
- You prefer gentler heat or want longer sessions

**Choose traditional if:**

- You want 170°F+ heat and steam
- You plan to use it with family or friends
- You are installing outdoors or building a dedicated room and can add 240V wiring

**Hybrid saunas** combine a traditional heater with infrared panels. They cost more but let you switch between both experiences.
