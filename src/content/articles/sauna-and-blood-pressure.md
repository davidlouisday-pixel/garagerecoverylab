This guide is based on the studies listed at the end. It is general information, not medical advice. If you have high or low blood pressure, or take medication for it, talk to your doctor before using a sauna.

## The short answer

| Question | What the research shows |
| --- | --- |
| What does one session do? | Blood pressure drops during and right after, and systolic pressure stays lower for at least 30 minutes |
| What about regular use? | 4 to 7 sessions a week was linked to about half the risk of developing hypertension over about 25 years |
| Is that proof sauna prevents hypertension? | No. It is an observational study of Finnish men |
| Who should be careful? | Uncontrolled high blood pressure, low blood pressure on standing, and anyone drinking alcohol |

## What one session does

The clearest direct evidence comes from a 2018 study in the *Journal of Human Hypertension* by Laukkanen and colleagues in Jyväskylä, Finland. **102 adults** (56 men and 46 women, average age about 52), each with at least one heart disease risk factor, took a single **30-minute** traditional sauna session at about **163°F (73°C)** with **10 to 20% humidity**. They had a short warm shower halfway through.

| Measure | Before | Right after | 30 minutes later |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Systolic blood pressure | 137 mmHg | 130 mmHg | 130 mmHg |
| Diastolic blood pressure | 82 mmHg | 75 mmHg | 81 mmHg |
| Heart rate (median) | 64 bpm | 79 bpm | 65 bpm |
| Pulse wave velocity (artery stiffness) | 9.8 m/s | 8.6 m/s | 9.0 m/s |

So systolic pressure fell by about **4%** and stayed down through the 30-minute recovery period, diastolic pressure dipped and then mostly came back, and arteries were measurably less stiff. Heart rate rose during the heat, roughly as it would with light exercise, then settled.

The authors noted the limits plainly: there was **no control group**, it was a **single session**, and the results apply to a hot, dry Finnish sauna, not to cooler saunas or hot water.

## What regular use is linked to

The long-term evidence comes from the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease study in eastern Finland. Zaccardi and colleagues (*American Journal of Hypertension*, 2017) followed **1,621 men aged 42 to 60** who had normal blood pressure at the start, for a median of **24.7 years**. During that time **251** of them developed high blood pressure.

Compared with men who used a sauna once a week:

- **2 to 3 sessions a week:** about **17% lower risk** in the fully adjusted model, but that result was not statistically clear.
- **4 to 7 sessions a week:** about **47% lower risk** in the fully adjusted model (hazard ratio 0.53).

The university's press release rounded this to "nearly 50%" lower risk. You may also see 24% and 46% quoted. Those figures come from a less-adjusted model in the same paper.

The authors listed real limits:

- It shows an **association**, not cause and effect. Sauna habits may track other healthy habits.
- Sauna use was asked about **once**, at the start, and habits can change over 25 years.
- Only **middle-aged Finnish men** were studied, and the results "cannot be directly applied" to steam rooms, hot tubs or cooler saunas.

## Why heat might help

Heat widens blood vessels, which lowers resistance to blood flow, and the 2018 study saw arteries become less stiff after a session. The researchers suggest that repeating that effect several times a week could, over years, help keep blood pressure lower. That mechanism is a reasonable explanation, not something these studies proved.

## Using a sauna safely with blood pressure concerns

The 2018 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* review by the same Finnish group says sauna use is safe for most people when done sensibly. It also lists reasons to avoid the sauna or check with a doctor first, including:

- **Uncontrolled high blood pressure**
- **Unstable angina, a recent heart attack or severe aortic stenosis**
- **Decompensated heart failure**

Practical steps that follow from the studies above:

1. **Stand up slowly.** Heat widens blood vessels, so standing quickly after a session can make you light-headed. The hypertension researchers specifically flag caution for people with orthostatic hypotension.
2. **Skip alcohol.** The Mayo Clinic Proceedings review warns that alcohol plus sauna raises the risk of low blood pressure, heart rhythm problems and falls.
3. **Keep it moderate.** The single-session study used 30 minutes at about 163°F with a break. Our guide to [how long to stay in a sauna](/how-long-should-you-stay-in-a-sauna/) covers typical session lengths.
4. **Drink water.** You lose fluid through sweat, and dehydration lowers blood pressure further.
5. **Do not stop medication** or change your treatment because of sauna use.

## Cole's experience

Cole uses his gym's sauna **four or more times a week**, for about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, right after training. He has not measured his blood pressure before and after, so this guide does not include a personal blood pressure claim.
