This guide is based on the research and safety sources listed at the end. Cole has not reviewed a massage chair hands-on, so there is no first-hand section here.

## The short answer

| Question | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Can a massage chair reduce stress? | Yes, in one small 2025 randomized trial |
| Can it ease neck and back pain? | Yes, in the same trial; broader massage research suggests short-term relief |
| Does it replace a massage therapist or treatment? | No good evidence either way; do not use it to delay seeing a doctor |
| Is it safe? | For most adults, yes. Specific conditions and children are the exceptions |

## The best direct evidence: a 2025 trial

Most massage research involves people, not machines. The most useful study on chairs themselves is a 2025 randomized controlled trial published in *Health Science Reports* by researchers at Universiti Sains Malaysia.

- **Who:** 24 full-time hospital staff, including nurses and lab technologists, chosen because they had high stress, anxiety or depression scores on a screening questionnaire.
- **What:** half used an automated massage chair for **15 minutes, 3 times a week, for 4 weeks** (12 sessions). The other half did audio-guided progressive muscle relaxation in the same chair with the massage switched off.
- **Results in the massage chair group:**
  - **Stress** scores dropped after 6 sessions and stayed lower after 12.
  - **Depression** scores dropped after 6 sessions.
  - **Neck pain** and **lower back pain** dropped after 6 and 12 sessions.
  - **Calf pain** dropped after 12 sessions.
  - **Anxiety, blood pressure and heart rate** did not change significantly.

The authors concluded the chair "significantly alleviated" depression, stress and musculoskeletal pain. The limits matter: 24 people is a small trial, it lasted only four weeks, and the participants were hospital workers selected for high stress scores.

## What broader massage research says

The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) summarizes research on massage in general, mostly hands-on massage:

- **Neck and shoulder pain:** massage may help, though benefits may be short-term.
- **Low back pain:** only weak evidence.
- **Fibromyalgia:** may help with pain, anxiety and depression if continued for at least 5 weeks.
- **Headaches:** inconsistent results from a small number of studies.

A chair is not the same as a therapist, so treat these as a rough guide to what massage can do rather than proof for any particular chair.

## Safety: who should not use one

NCCIH says the risk of harm from massage "appears to be low", but rare serious problems, such as blood clots, nerve injury and fractures, have been reported, mostly with vigorous massage or in higher-risk people such as older adults.

Massage chair manuals list their own warnings. One manufacturer's warning sheet says **not to use** the chair with:

- Circulation problems such as **varicose veins or thrombosis**
- A suspected **acute neck sprain** (whiplash)
- A doctor's advice against it

It advises **checking with a doctor first** for a serious heart problem, serious diabetes, serious osteoporosis, a high fever, pregnancy, a pacemaker or other electronic medical device, spine abnormalities or curvature, and a history of vertigo or fainting.

### Children

Massage chairs have strong motors and moving parts. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission estimated at least **2,200 emergency room visits** involving massage chairs between 2012 and 2021. In 2013, a 4-year-old boy was killed after crawling under a chair that was switched on with the remote. Keep children away from the chair, and keep the remote out of reach.

## Getting the most from one

1. **Start gentle and short.** The trial used 15-minute sessions. Use a lower intensity first and see how you feel the next day.
2. **Be consistent.** The trial's benefits showed up over 6 to 12 sessions, not after one.
3. **Pair it with recovery habits.** If you use heat as well, our guide to [sauna for muscle recovery](/sauna-for-muscle-recovery/) covers what the research says about heat after training.
4. **Do not use it to put off a diagnosis.** NCCIH specifically advises against using massage to postpone seeing a health care provider about pain.
