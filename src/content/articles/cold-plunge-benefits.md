This guide is based on published studies and reviews, listed at the end. Cole does not cold plunge himself, so nothing here is first-hand.

## The short answer

| Claimed benefit | How strong is the evidence? |
| --- | --- |
| Less muscle soreness after exercise | **Moderate.** Several reviews agree, but the effect is small and the trials are low quality. |
| Hormone and alertness response | **Strong for the short-term response.** Noradrenaline rose about 530% in cold water. |
| Fewer sick days | **Limited.** One large trial of cold showers, with a twist. |
| Brown fat and metabolism | **Early.** Small observational study of winter swimmers. |
| Muscle growth | **Negative if done right after lifting.** |

## 1. Less muscle soreness

This is the best-studied benefit.

- A **2022 Cochrane review** of **17 small trials with 366 people** found "some evidence" that cold water immersion reduces delayed-onset muscle soreness compared with rest. It rated the quality of the evidence as low.
- A **2016 meta-analysis** of **9 randomized trials with 169 people** found the best results at **52 to 59°F (11 to 15°C)** for **11 to 15 minutes**, and described cold water as "slightly better" than passive recovery.

So it works, a little. If soreness is stopping you from training, a plunge may take the edge off. See [cold plunge temperature and time](/cold-plunge-temperature/) for the doses those studies used.

## 2. The catch: it can blunt muscle gains

This is the result most cold plunge marketing leaves out.

In a 2015 study in *The Journal of Physiology*, Roberts and colleagues had **21 physically active men** strength train **twice a week for 12 weeks**. After each session, half sat in cold water for **10 minutes** and half did a light active cool-down.

The active cool-down group gained **more muscle mass and more strength**. Several measures, including type II muscle fiber size (up 17%), only increased in the active group. A second experiment found that cold water blunted the signaling that drives muscle growth after a workout. The authors concluded that using cold water as a regular recovery strategy after strength training "should be reconsidered".

**What to do with that:** if you lift for size or strength, avoid plunging right after your sessions. Plunge on rest days, or after cardio, or use heat instead. Our guide to [sauna for muscle recovery](/sauna-for-muscle-recovery/) covers what the post-workout heat research found.

## 3. A big noradrenaline spike

The alert, wired feeling after a plunge has a measurable basis. In a 2000 study by Šrámek and colleagues, young men sat in water up to the neck for an hour:

- At **57°F (14°C)**, blood **noradrenaline rose about 530%** and **dopamine about 250%**, and metabolic rate rose about **350%**.
- At **68°F (20°C)**, metabolic rate rose about 93%.

The authors attribute this mainly to activation of the sympathetic nervous system, the "fight or flight" system. That is a real, strong short-term response. An hour in a lab is far longer than a home plunge, and the study did not measure mood.

## 4. Fewer sick days, with a twist

The largest trial on cold water and illness used showers, not plunges. Buijze and colleagues randomized **3,018 adults** in the Netherlands to end their usual warm shower with **30, 60 or 90 seconds** of cold water for 30 days, or to shower normally.

- The cold shower groups had **29% fewer sickness absence days** from work.
- They did **not** have significantly fewer days of illness.

In other words, people got sick about as often but felt able to keep working. It is an interesting result, but it is not evidence that cold water strengthens your immune system.

## 5. Brown fat and metabolism

A 2021 study by Søberg and colleagues compared young male winter swimmers, who averaged about **11 minutes a week in cold water** and **57 minutes in a sauna**, with men who did neither. The swimmers produced more heat when cooled.

That was a small observational study of 7 to 8 swimmers, all men, so it cannot show that cold water caused the difference. It is not evidence that home plunges cause fat loss.

## The risks

A 2017 review by Tipton and colleagues, "Cold water immersion: kill or cure?", notes that cold water immersion is a known precursor to drowning, cardiac arrest and hypothermia, and that support for many claimed benefits remains "varied", sometimes no more than anecdote.

- **Never plunge alone** or after alcohol.
- **Talk to a doctor first** if you have heart disease, high blood pressure or a rhythm problem. The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review says a cold plunge straight after a sauna is not usually recommended for people with heart disease.
- **Colder is not better.** The soreness research found no extra benefit below about 50°F.

## Is a home cold plunge worth it?

If you want the soreness benefit and enjoy the ritual, the evidence supports occasional use at moderate cold. If you are mainly chasing muscle growth, the research points the other way after lifting sessions.
