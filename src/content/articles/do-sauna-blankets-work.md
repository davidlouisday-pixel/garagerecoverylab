## What a sauna blanket is

A sauna blanket is a padded sleeping-bag-style wrap with heating elements inside, usually marketed as **infrared**. You lie down, zip it up to your neck and run it for a set time and temperature. Your head stays out, so you breathe room-temperature air.

That last point is the biggest difference from a traditional sauna, where hot air surrounds your whole body, and from an infrared cabin, where you sit inside a heated room. See [infrared vs traditional saunas](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/) for how those two compare.

## What the research actually covers

### Traditional saunas, used for years

The headline sauna findings come from the **Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study (KIHD)** in Finland. It followed middle-aged men using traditional saunas over decades and linked more frequent use with lower rates of cardiovascular death and other outcomes. The 2018 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* review by Laukkanen and colleagues summarizes that work. It is observational, in men, in a country where saunas are part of daily life.

None of that tested a heated blanket.

### Dry sauna studies in general

A 2018 systematic review by Hussain and Cohen looked at clinical studies of regular dry sauna bathing. Its conclusion:

- Regular dry sauna bathing **has potential health benefits**.
- Few studies were randomized controlled trials, and **most were small (under 40 people)**.
- More and better data are needed on side effects, and on the best frequency and duration for different types of sauna.

If the evidence for whole-room saunas is still called "potential," the evidence for blankets, which have not been studied directly as far as we could find, is weaker still. For infrared specifically, see our [infrared sauna benefits](/infrared-sauna-benefits/) guide.

## The weight loss claim

Sauna blanket marketing often mentions weight loss. What actually happens:

- You **sweat out water**, so the scale drops right after.
- You **regain it** when you drink, which you should.
- Sitting in heat raises your heart rate, but the calories burned are small next to exercise or diet.

We cover this in detail in [sauna for weight loss](/sauna-for-weight-loss/).

## Who a sauna blanket suits

**Good fit if you:**

- want a warm, sweaty wind-down at home
- have no space for a sauna and no gym sauna nearby
- are happy with a relaxation tool rather than a proven health intervention

**Look elsewhere if you:**

- want the experience the long-term research is based on (a traditional sauna at 160 to 195°F)
- want to sit up, read or share the session
- train at a gym that already has a sauna

## Using one safely

- Follow the maker's **time and temperature limits**. Start low and short.
- **Drink water** before and after.
- **Do not fall asleep** in it.
- **No alcohol.** The Mayo Clinic Proceedings review warns alcohol with sauna raises the risk of low blood pressure and falls.
- Ask a doctor first if you have **heart disease, low or high blood pressure**, or are **pregnant**.
- Stop if you feel dizzy, sick or overheated.

## Cole's take

Cole's sauna is a big electric unit with rocks at his gym, about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, four or more times a week. He has never used a sauna blanket, so he cannot compare the two first-hand. If your goal is to copy what the research studied, he would point you toward a traditional sauna. If you just want a cheap way to sweat at home, a blanket is a reasonable thing to try.
