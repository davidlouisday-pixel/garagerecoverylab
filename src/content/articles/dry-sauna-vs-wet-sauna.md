This guide is based on the sources listed at the end. Cole uses a traditional electric sauna with rocks at his gym, the kind that can run dry or with water on the stones.

## The short answer

| | Dry sauna | Wet sauna (water on stones) | Steam room |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Air temperature | About 175 to 212°F (80 to 100°C) | Similar room, often run a bit cooler | About 122°F (50°C) |
| Humidity | About 10 to 20% | Rises with each ladle, then falls | Close to 100% |
| Heat source | Electric or wood stove with stones | Same stove, plus water on the stones | Steam generator |
| Feel | Hot, dry air | Bursts of intense, moist heat | Warm and very humid |

Temperature and humidity figures for dry saunas and steam rooms are from Columbia University's health service.

## "Wet sauna" means two different things

This is where most of the confusion comes from:

1. **A traditional sauna with water on the stones.** In Finland, ladling water onto the stones is a normal part of using a sauna. The steam is called löyly. The Finnish Sauna Society describes the heater's job as turning that water into steam.
2. **A steam room.** Some people call any humid hot room a wet sauna. A steam room is a different design: tiled, sealed, cooler and close to fully saturated with steam.

Most home and gym saunas with a stone heater are both "dry" and "wet": dry until you add water.

## What humidity does to your body

Sweat cools you only when it evaporates. Humid air slows that down, so the same temperature feels hotter and your body works harder.

A 2026 study from the University of Jyväskylä measured this in **50 adults** across four 10-minute sauna sessions in which people threw water on the stones freely. According to Harvia's summary of the study:

- Humidity averaged about **41%** across sessions, and about **54%** at sitting height.
- Each extra degree Celsius of air temperature added about **1.4 beats per minute** to heart rate.
- Each extra percentage point of humidity added about **0.37 beats per minute**.

The university's own release says heart rate rose from about **79 to 121 beats per minute** over ten minutes, and that humidity raised both heart rate and core temperature, while temperature stayed the biggest factor.

So adding steam is a real increase in effort, not just a feeling.

## Which one is closer to the research?

The large Finnish studies on heart health, blood pressure and dementia followed people using **traditional Finnish saunas**, where adding steam is normal. Those studies did not separate dry and wet use. Steam rooms and infrared saunas were not what those people used. If matching the research matters to you, a traditional sauna with stones is the closest fit, used dry or with steam as you like.

## How to choose

- **You want the hottest, most traditional session:** a traditional sauna with stones, and add steam when you want it.
- **You find dry heat harsh on your nose or throat:** try a little water on the stones, or a steam room.
- **You want lower heat:** a steam room runs about 122°F, and an infrared sauna runs about 110 to 135°F. See [infrared vs traditional sauna](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/).
- **You are building at home:** a stone heater gives you both dry and wet in one room. A steam room needs a sealed, waterproof enclosure and a steam generator.

## Tips for adding steam

1. **Use small amounts.** A ladle at a time is enough.
2. **Aim for the stones,** not the heater body or the controls.
3. **Ask first** in a shared sauna. Steam affects everyone in the room.
4. **Only do it on heaters made for it.** Never pour water in an infrared sauna.
