## The short answer

| Question | Answer |
| --- | --- |
| What is it? | A recline with your legs raised to about heart level |
| Does it make you weightless? | No. It spreads your weight across the chair |
| Is it from NASA? | Loosely, from NASA research on relaxed body posture in space |
| Is there a single correct angle? | No. NASA describes a range of postures |
| Does it have proven health effects? | Not in massage chair studies we could find |

## Where the idea comes from

When astronauts relax in microgravity, their bodies settle into a typical shape: hips and knees bent, arms floating up. NASA calls this the **neutral body posture**. According to a 2020 NASA Spinoff article, the agency first described it from **Skylab** photos, wrote it into its human factors standard in the late 1980s, and checked it again on a 1993 Space Shuttle mission.

The key finding, in NASA's words, is that "there is no universal posture the human body takes on in zero gravity." It is a range of postures with similar joint angles.

The same article describes how the massage chair maker LURACO built a zero gravity setting based on NASA's neutral body posture specifications. That is the link most "zero gravity" marketing points back to.

HowStuffWorks offers a different origin story: that the position mimics how astronauts sit during **launch**. Either way, the chair does not defy gravity. It just reclines you in a particular way.

## What the position does

In a zero gravity recline:

- **Your legs rise** to about the level of your heart, or slightly above.
- **Your weight shifts** from your seat into the backrest.
- **Your hips stay bent,** so your lower back is not arched.

The practical effect is that the back rollers press into you with more of your own weight behind them, which many people find feels deeper. Osaki, a chair maker, describes a torso-to-thigh angle of **about 120 degrees** as the sweet spot. NASA's summary gives no single angle, so treat any exact number as a maker's design choice.

## What the claims say, and what is proven

Chair makers often say zero gravity:

- Takes pressure off the spine
- Improves circulation
- Deepens relaxation and massage

Raising the legs above the heart is a common general recommendation for swelling and vein health, and HowStuffWorks notes this. But we found **no study testing whether a zero gravity setting on a massage chair improves health outcomes** compared with a normal recline. Specific percentages you may see, such as large reductions in spinal compression or muscle soreness, appear on maker sites without a traceable study.

For what massage chairs themselves have been shown to do, see our guide to [whether massage chairs are good for you](/are-massage-chairs-good-for-you/). The best direct trial found modest drops in stress and neck and back pain.

## What to check before you buy

Zero gravity is now standard on most mid-range and premium chairs, so the details matter more than the label:

1. **How many recline positions.** Many chairs offer more than one zero gravity stage. Check how far each one goes.
2. **Wall clearance.** A full recline needs space behind the chair. Check the listing for the distance needed from the wall, or whether it is a space-saving design that slides forward.
3. **Fit for your height and weight.** Raised legs only help if your calves sit in the leg rest properly. Check the maker's height and weight range.
4. **Whether the massage still works fully reclined.** Some programs are designed for a specific angle.
5. **Getting in and out.** If you have mobility limits, check how far up the chair returns.

## Who should be careful

Massage chair warnings commonly list circulation problems such as varicose veins or blood clots, a pacemaker, pregnancy, recent surgery, serious osteoporosis and spine problems. If any apply to you, check with a doctor before using a massage chair in any position. Keep children away from chairs, including when they recline.
