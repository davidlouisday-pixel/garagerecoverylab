This guide is based on the research and public health sources listed at the end. Cole uses a sauna at his gym four or more times a week. He does not own a hot tub, so the hot tub side is research only.

## The short answer

| | Sauna | Hot tub |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | 150 to 195°F air (traditional) | Water at 104°F maximum |
| Long-term health research | Strong: Finnish studies over 20+ years | Limited: small lab trials |
| Maintenance | Wipe down benches, occasional cleaning | Test water often, sanitizer, filters, water changes |
| Running cost | Only when in use | Keeps water hot all the time |
| Indoors in a garage | Common | Harder: water weight, humidity, drainage |
| Social use | 1 to 6 people, quiet | Better for groups and conversation |

## The health research

### Sauna

The best-known evidence is the Kuopio study in eastern Finland. In **2,315 middle-aged men** followed for about **20 years**, 4 to 7 sauna sessions a week was linked to about a **63% lower risk of sudden cardiac death** and a **66% lower risk of dementia** than one session a week. Related papers linked frequent use with lower rates of stroke, high blood pressure and pneumonia. These are observational studies, so they show an association, not proof. We cover them in [how often should you use a sauna](/how-often-should-you-use-a-sauna/).

### Hot tub

There is no comparable long-term study of hot tub users. The closest evidence is lab research on hot water immersion.

In a 2016 study in *The Journal of Physiology*, Brunt and colleagues had **20 young, sedentary adults** sit in either a hot bath or a lukewarm one, **4 to 5 times a week for 8 weeks**. The hot bath group soaked in **105°F (40.5°C)** water, enough to keep their core temperature at or above 101.3°F for **60 minutes** a session. Compared with the lukewarm group, they had:

- Better blood vessel function (flow-mediated dilation rose from **5.6% to 10.9%**)
- Less arterial stiffness (aortic pulse wave velocity fell from **7.1 to 6.1 m/s**)
- Lower average blood pressure (mean arterial pressure fell from **83 to 78 mmHg**)

That is a promising result, but note the details: it was small, it was supervised, and the water was **hotter than the 104°F** consumer limit for hot tubs, for a full hour at a time. A typical 15 to 20 minute soak at 100 to 102°F is a much smaller dose.

## Safety

**Hot tubs:**

- The **CDC** says hot tub water should not exceed **104°F** to prevent scalding. The **CPSC** gives the same limit, and describes **100°F** as safe for a healthy adult.
- The CPSC warns that soaking in water above **102°F** can harm a fetus in the first three months of pregnancy, and advises pregnant women to stay at **100°F** or below.
- Alcohol plus hot water can cause drowsiness, unconsciousness and drowning, according to the CPSC.
- Germs are a real issue. The CDC notes that hot tub water sits in the ideal range for *Legionella* growth (**77 to 113°F**) and has been linked to outbreaks of Legionnaires' disease. Keeping sanitizer levels right is what controls it.

**Saunas:** the 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review calls sauna bathing safe for most people when used sensibly. Reasons to avoid it or see a doctor first include unstable angina, a recent heart attack, severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled high blood pressure and fever. Cleveland Clinic adds pregnancy and alcohol.

## Upkeep

This is the biggest practical difference.

A sauna is a dry wooden room. You wipe the benches, sweep the floor and occasionally clean the wood.

A hot tub is a body of warm water shared by bathers. The CDC's guidance for public hot tubs gives a sense of what control requires: free chlorine of **3 to 10 ppm** or bromine of **4 to 8 ppm**, pH of **7.2 to 7.8**, and testing at least twice a day under heavy use. A home tub needs less frequent testing than a public spa, but the same chemistry.

## Which to choose

- **Choose a sauna** if the long-term health research is your main reason, you want low upkeep, or you are building in a garage.
- **Choose a hot tub** if you want something social, enjoy floating in water, or find water easier on sore joints than dry heat.
