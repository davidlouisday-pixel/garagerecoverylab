This guide pulls together the research behind the sauna guides on this site. Every figure links to its source at the end. Cole uses an electric rock-heater sauna at his gym four or more times a week, and his experience is labeled separately below.

## The short answer

| Benefit | Strength of evidence | Key finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart health | **Strong association** | 63% lower sudden cardiac death at 4 to 7 sessions a week |
| Lower blood pressure, short term | **Experimental** | Systolic fell from 137 to 130 mmHg after one session |
| Lower blood pressure, long term | **Association** | About 46% lower risk of high blood pressure at 4 to 7 sessions |
| Brain health | **Strong association** | 66% lower dementia risk at 4 to 7 sessions |
| Stroke | **Association** | 61% lower stroke risk at 4 to 7 sessions |
| Lungs | **Association** | 41% fewer respiratory illnesses at 4 or more sessions |
| Muscle recovery | **Small trials** | Better recovery and less soreness after resistance training |
| Weight loss | **Not supported** | The weight lost is sweat |

Almost all the long-term numbers come from the Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease Risk Factor study in eastern Finland, which recorded how often middle-aged people used a sauna and followed them for 15 to 25 years. Each comparison is against people who used a sauna once a week.

## 1. Heart health

The headline study is Laukkanen and colleagues, *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2015. It followed **2,315 men aged 42 to 60** for a median of **20.7 years**.

- 4 to 7 sessions a week was linked to about a **63% lower risk of sudden cardiac death** than once a week.
- Fatal heart disease and death from any cause followed the same pattern.
- Sessions of **more than 19 minutes** were linked to better outcomes than sessions under 11 minutes.

The saunas averaged about **174°F** for about **14 minutes**. For what that means for your own settings, see [how hot should a sauna be](/how-hot-should-a-sauna-be/) and [how long to stay in a sauna](/how-long-should-you-stay-in-a-sauna/).

## 2. Blood pressure

**After one session.** A 2018 experiment in the *Journal of Human Hypertension* put **102 adults** with at least one heart risk factor through a **30-minute** session at about **163°F**. Average systolic blood pressure fell from **137 to 130 mmHg**, diastolic from **82 to 75 mmHg**, and arterial stiffness dropped. This is direct experimental evidence, though for a single session.

**Over decades.** In **1,621 men** without high blood pressure at the start, followed for a median of **24.7 years**, 4 to 7 sessions a week was linked to about **46% lower risk** of developing it. The 2 to 3 sessions group showed a smaller reduction that was not statistically clear (*American Journal of Hypertension*, 2017).

## 3. Brain health

In the same 2,315 men as the heart study, 4 to 7 sessions a week was linked to a **66% lower risk of dementia** and a **65% lower risk of Alzheimer's disease** (*Age and Ageing*, 2017).

## 4. Stroke

In **1,628 men and women aged 53 to 74** followed for 15 years, people who used a sauna 4 to 7 times a week were **61% less likely to have a stroke** than once-a-week users, according to the University of Eastern Finland. This is one of the few results that included women.

## 5. Lungs

In **1,935 men** followed for a median of **25.6 years**, 2 to 3 sessions a week was linked to about **27% fewer** hospital-diagnosed respiratory illnesses such as COPD, asthma and pneumonia, and 4 or more sessions to about **41% fewer**. A separate analysis found about **44% lower pneumonia risk** at 4 or more sessions a week.

The full frequency breakdown is in [how often should you use a sauna](/how-often-should-you-use-a-sauna/).

## 6. Muscle recovery

The recovery research is newer and much smaller, and mostly uses infrared saunas. A 2023 trial found a post-workout infrared session improved recovery of performance and reduced soreness after resistance training. A 2015 trial of 10 men found slightly better jump performance after an endurance session. We cover both, and the timing question, in [sauna for muscle recovery](/sauna-for-muscle-recovery/) and [sauna before or after a workout](/sauna-before-or-after-workout/).

Heat is a useful contrast to cold here. A 12-week trial found regular cold plunges straight after strength training reduced muscle and strength gains. See [cold plunge benefits](/cold-plunge-benefits/).

## 7. Conditions studied in smaller trials

A 2018 systematic review by Hussain and Cohen found **40 clinical studies with 3,855 participants** on regular dry sauna use. Small studies reported benefits in heart failure, chronic pain, chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia, rheumatoid arthritis, COPD and allergic rhinitis, among others. Only 13 studies were randomized, and most were small, so the authors called these "potential" benefits that need better data. Much of this work used infrared saunas; see [infrared sauna benefits](/infrared-sauna-benefits/).

## What sauna use does not do

- **Weight loss.** In a study of 674 students, a 20-minute session took off about 0.8 lb for women and 1.1 lb for men, almost all of it sweat. See [sauna for weight loss](/sauna-for-weight-loss/).
- **Prove cause and effect.** The long-term results are observational. People who sauna most days may differ in health, income and free time in ways the researchers could not fully adjust for.
- **Carry over automatically.** The long-term studies were mostly middle-aged Finnish men in traditional saunas. The results may differ for women, younger people, infrared saunas or [steam rooms](/sauna-vs-steam-room/).

## Cole's experience

Cole uses his gym's sauna **four or more times a week**, for about **20 minutes** at **180 to 190°F**, right after training. He describes coming out relaxed, clear-minded and less stiff, and finds he sleeps more easily. That is one person's experience, not a study result.

## Who should be careful

The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review, written by the Finnish research group, calls sauna bathing safe for most people when used sensibly, including many people with stable heart disease. It lists reasons to avoid the sauna or check with a doctor first:

- Unstable angina, a recent heart attack, or severe aortic stenosis
- Uncontrolled high blood pressure or decompensated heart failure
- Fever or acute infection

It also warns that **alcohol plus sauna** raises the risk of low blood pressure, heart problems and falls. Cleveland Clinic adds that you should talk to a doctor first if you are pregnant, and lists dehydration as one of the main risks, so drink water before and after.

## Getting the benefits at home

The research points to frequency more than intensity: regular sessions in a traditional sauna in the 170s °F for 15 to 20 minutes.
