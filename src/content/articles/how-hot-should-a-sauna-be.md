This guide is based on published studies and medical reviews, listed at the end. Cole uses an electric sauna with a rock heater at his gym, and his routine is labeled separately below.

## The short answer

| Sauna type | Typical temperature | What the research used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional (Finnish), dry | 150 to 195°F | Average about 174°F in the 20-year Finnish study |
| Traditional, at the hot end | Up to about 212°F at head height | 176 to 212°F, per a 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review |
| Infrared | 110 to 150°F | About 109 to 122°F in post-workout recovery trials |

If you are new, start at the bottom of those ranges for 5 to 10 minutes. If you want the sessions the Finnish research describes, a traditional sauna in the 170s °F with low humidity is the closest match.

## What the Finnish study actually measured

The study most people have heard of is Laukkanen and colleagues, published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* in 2015. It followed **2,315 men aged 42 to 60** in eastern Finland for a median of **20.7 years**.

What the saunas were like:

- **Average temperature:** 78.9°C, which is about **174°F**. The range was 104 to 212°F.
- **Average session:** about **14 minutes**.
- **Average use:** about **2 sessions a week**.

What it found: men who used a sauna **4 to 7 times a week** had about a **63% lower risk of sudden cardiac death** than men who went once a week, after adjusting for other risk factors. The paper reports similar lower risks for fatal heart disease and death from any cause. Men whose sessions lasted **more than 19 minutes** also did better than those under 11 minutes.

Later papers from the same research group found similar patterns:

- **Dementia (2017, *Age and Ageing*).** In the same 2,315 men, 4 to 7 sessions a week was linked with a **66% lower risk of dementia** and a **65% lower risk of Alzheimer's disease** than once a week.
- **Stroke (2018, *Neurology*).** In **1,628 men and women aged 53 to 74** followed for 15 years, people who used a sauna 4 to 7 times a week were **61% less likely to have a stroke** than once-a-week users, according to the University of Eastern Finland.

### What it does not prove

These are observational studies. They show that frequent sauna users had better outcomes, not that the sauna caused them. People who are healthy enough, and have the time, to sauna most days may differ in other ways. The main cohort was also middle-aged Finnish men using traditional saunas, so results may not carry over to women, other ages or infrared saunas.

One more thing matters for this guide: **the study grouped people by how often and how long they went, not by temperature.** It cannot tell you whether 190°F beats 170°F.

## What happens in your body at sauna heat

A 2018 experiment in the *Journal of Human Hypertension* put **102 adults** with at least one heart risk factor through a single **30-minute session at about 163°F (73°C)** with 10 to 20% humidity. Right after the session:

- Average systolic blood pressure fell from **137 to 130 mmHg**, and diastolic from **82 to 75 mmHg**.
- Arterial stiffness, measured as pulse wave velocity, dropped as well.

Systolic pressure was still lower 30 minutes after they got out. This was one session, not a long-term trial, but it shows that a moderate traditional sauna temperature is enough to produce a measurable effect.

## Traditional vs infrared temperatures

**Traditional saunas** heat the air with a stove or electric heater and stones. Cleveland Clinic gives a typical range of **150 to 195°F**. The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review describes Finnish saunas as **176 to 212°F** at head height, with **10 to 20% relative humidity**.

**Infrared saunas** warm your skin directly with infrared panels, so the room runs cooler, usually **110 to 150°F**. The recovery trials in our [sauna for muscle recovery](/sauna-for-muscle-recovery/) guide used infrared saunas at about **109°F** and **122°F**.

Almost all of the long-term heart research used traditional saunas. If those studies are why you want a sauna, that is worth weighing. Our [infrared vs traditional sauna](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/) guide compares the two on heat-up time, wiring and cost.

## Humidity: why the same temperature can feel very different

A Finnish sauna is a dry heat. Pouring water on the stones (called *löyly* in Finnish) briefly raises the humidity, and your skin feels much hotter even though the thermometer barely moves. If 190°F feels fine dry but brutal after a ladle of water, that is normal. Add water a little at a time.

## Cole's experience

Cole uses his gym's sauna, a large electric heater with rocks, at **180 to 190°F** for about **20 minutes**, four or more times a week, right after training. That is hotter than the 174°F average in the Finnish study and well within the traditional range in the Mayo review.

In his words, he comes out "loose and less stiff" and "clear minded," and sleeps more easily. That is one person's experience, not a study result. He did not start at 190°F, and nobody needs to.

## How to set your sauna temperature

1. **Start low.** Try 150 to 160°F traditional or 110 to 120°F infrared, for 5 to 10 minutes.
2. **Change one thing at a time.** Add a few minutes per session before you add heat.
3. **Aim for a sustainable session.** For most people that is 15 to 20 minutes. See [how long to stay in a sauna](/how-long-should-you-stay-in-a-sauna/).
4. **Let a traditional heater fully preheat.** A larger traditional heater can take 30 minutes or more to reach temperature. Our [sauna running cost calculator](/sauna-running-cost-calculator/) shows what that preheat costs on your electricity rate.
5. **Get out early if you feel dizzy, sick or get a headache.** Temperature targets are not worth pushing through that.

## Who should be careful

The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings review calls sauna bathing safe for most people, including people with stable heart disease, when it is used sensibly. It lists these as reasons to avoid the sauna or check with a doctor first:

- Unstable angina, a recent heart attack, or severe aortic stenosis
- Uncontrolled high blood pressure or decompensated heart failure
- A fever or acute infection

The review also warns that **alcohol plus sauna** raises the risk of low blood pressure, heart problems and falls. It says jumping into **cold water right after** a sauna is not usually recommended for people with heart disease. Cleveland Clinic adds that you should talk to a doctor first if you are pregnant.
