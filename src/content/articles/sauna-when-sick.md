This guide is based on the studies listed at the end. It is general information, not medical advice. If you are unsure whether you are well enough for heat, ask your doctor.

## The short answer

| Question | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Does a sauna cure a cold? | No good study shows that it shortens one |
| Can regular sauna use prevent colds? | One small 1990 trial says possibly |
| Is regular use linked to less pneumonia? | Yes, in a long Finnish study of men |
| Sauna with a fever? | Skip it |
| Sauna with a mild head cold? | A short, cooler session is usually fine if you feel up to it |

## Prevention: the evidence is better than for cure

### The 1990 common cold trial

In 1990, Edzard Ernst and colleagues published a small trial in *Annals of Medicine*. **25 volunteers** used a sauna regularly and **25 controls** did not. The team tracked how often people caught colds, how long the colds lasted and how bad they were, for **six months**.

- The sauna group had **significantly fewer colds**.
- In the **last three months**, the sauna group had roughly **half as many** colds as the controls.
- Colds lasted about as long and felt about as bad in both groups.

The authors' own conclusion was careful: regular sauna use "probably" reduces colds, and more studies are needed. Ernst later noted that the trial was small and was not randomized. Treat it as a hint, not proof.

### The Finnish pneumonia and lung disease data

The Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study (KIHD) followed middle-aged men in eastern Finland for decades. Two analyses from 2017 looked at the lungs:

- **Pneumonia.** Among **2,210 men** aged 42 to 61, followed for a median of **25.6 years**, there were **375** hospital-diagnosed cases of pneumonia. Compared with men who used a sauna once a week or less, the risk was **31 percent lower** with 2 to 3 sessions a week and **44 percent lower** with 4 or more, after adjusting for major risk factors (hazard ratios 0.69 and 0.56).
- **Respiratory disease overall** (COPD, asthma or pneumonia). Among **1,935 men**, the risk was **27 percent lower** with 2 to 3 sessions a week and **41 percent lower** with 4 or more (hazard ratios 0.73 and 0.59).

These are observational findings in Finnish men using traditional saunas. Men who use a sauna often may differ in other ways, and the researchers adjusted for what they could measure. It shows a link, not cause and effect.

## Using a sauna when you are already sick

We could not find a good trial testing whether a sauna helps once a cold has started. So the practical advice comes down to how sick you are.

### Probably fine: a mild head cold

If you have a runny or stuffy nose and a mild sore throat but otherwise feel normal:

1. **Keep it short.** 5 to 10 minutes instead of your usual session.
2. **Lower the heat.** Sit on a lower bench where the air is cooler.
3. **Drink water** before and after. Being sick and sweating both cost fluid.
4. **Get out if you feel dizzy, weak or worse.**
5. **Stay home from a shared sauna.** Colds spread in small enclosed rooms.

Warm air may make a stuffy nose feel better for a while. That is comfort, not a cure.

### Skip it: fever and whole-body symptoms

Skip the sauna if you have:

- a **fever** or chills
- **body aches** or feel wiped out
- **vomiting or diarrhea**, which already dehydrate you
- **chest symptoms** such as wheezing, a deep cough or shortness of breath

A fever already raises your core temperature and heart rate. A sauna piles more heat and sweat loss on top of that. Wait until you are fever-free and feel close to normal, then start back with shorter sessions. The 2018 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* sauna review also warns against combining sauna with alcohol, which some people reach for when they feel rough.

## Cole's experience

Cole uses his gym's sauna **four or more times a week** for about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, right after training. That is a habit for feeling loose and clear-headed, not a cold remedy, and we have no data on whether it has changed how often he gets sick. One person's routine is not evidence.
