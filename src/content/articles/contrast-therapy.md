This guide is based on the studies listed at the end. A note on experience: Cole uses a sauna four or more times a week, but he **does not cold plunge**, so everything about the cold side here comes from research, not from him.

## The short answer

| Question | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| Does it reduce soreness after exercise? | Yes, compared with doing nothing |
| Is it better than cold water alone? | Not clearly |
| Does it hurt muscle growth? | The cold part may, if used right after lifting |
| Best order? | No strong research answer; most protocols repeat 3 to 4 rounds |
| Who should be careful? | Heart disease, high blood pressure, pregnancy |

## What contrast therapy is

Contrast therapy alternates heat and cold. At home or at a gym, that usually means:

1. A **sauna** or hot tub for several minutes
2. A **cold plunge**, cold shower or ice bath for a short time
3. Repeat for a few rounds

In research, it is usually called **contrast water therapy (CWT)**: people switch between a hot bath and a cold bath, often for one to a few minutes each.

## What the research shows

### Soreness and strength after exercise

The best summary we found is a 2013 systematic review and meta-analysis by Bieuzen and colleagues in *PLoS One*. The authors concluded:

- Contrast water therapy **reduced muscle soreness and strength loss** after exercise compared with **passive recovery or rest**.
- Compared with **cold water immersion, warm water immersion, compression, active recovery and stretching**, there was **little evidence** that contrast therapy was better.
- The effects may matter most for **elite athletes** who compete again within a day or two.

In plain terms: it works better than sitting on the couch, but it is not magic, and other recovery methods do about as well.

### The catch for lifters: cold after strength training

If your goal is muscle size and strength, timing matters.

- **Roberts et al., 2015.** 21 active men strength-trained twice a week for **12 weeks**, with either **10 minutes of cold water immersion** or active recovery after each session. Strength and muscle mass increased **more in the active recovery group**.
- **Malta et al., 2021.** A meta-analysis concluded that regular cold water immersion used alongside exercise programs **has a harmful effect on resistance training adaptations**, but did not appear to affect aerobic performance.

These studies tested cold water, not saunas. We will cover the timing question in more detail in a guide on whether to cold plunge before or after a workout, coming later this month.

### What about long-term health?

The long-term health data people quote for saunas, such as the Finnish KIHD cohort, is about **regular sauna use**, not contrast therapy. We cover that in [sauna benefits](/sauna-benefits/). We did not find comparable long-term data for alternating heat and cold.

## How to try it

There is no single proven protocol. This is a cautious starting point based on common research setups, not a tested sauna-and-plunge prescription:

1. **Sauna, 10 to 15 minutes.** Long enough to feel properly warm. See [how long to stay in a sauna](/how-long-should-you-stay-in-a-sauna/).
2. **Cold, 30 seconds to 2 minutes.** Start with a cold shower. If you use a plunge, our [cold plunge temperature](/cold-plunge-temperature/) guide covers how cold to go.
3. **Rest a few minutes** at room temperature.
4. **Repeat 2 to 3 more rounds.**
5. **Drink water** before and after.

Safety rules that matter more than the protocol:

- **Never plunge alone**, and get in slowly. Sudden cold triggers a gasp reflex and a sharp rise in heart rate and blood pressure.
- **No alcohol.** The 2018 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* sauna review warns that alcohol with sauna raises the risk of low blood pressure and falls.
- **Ask a doctor first** if you have heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure or are pregnant.
- **Lifting for size?** Keep the cold part away from your strength sessions, for example on rest days or at least several hours later.

## Cole's take

Cole's routine is sauna only: about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, right after training, four or more times a week. He says it leaves him loose and clear-headed. He has not tried contrast therapy, so he cannot tell you how the cold side feels.
