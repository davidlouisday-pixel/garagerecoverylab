This guide is based on published studies and reviews, listed at the end. Cole does not cold plunge himself, so nothing here is first-hand.

## The short answer

| Goal | Temperature in the research | Time in the research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Less muscle soreness after hard exercise | 52 to 59°F (11 to 15°C) | 11 to 15 minutes |
| "Cold water immersion" in the Cochrane review | Below 59°F (15°C) | Varied by trial |
| Habit of experienced winter swimmers | Cold open water | About 11 minutes per week in total |
| Starting out (our suggestion, not a study dose) | 55 to 60°F | 1 to 3 minutes |

## What the soreness research found

The most useful study for this question is a 2016 meta-analysis in *Sports Medicine* by Machado and colleagues. It pooled **9 randomized trials with 169 people**, mostly active adults and athletes, that compared cold water immersion with simply resting after exercise.

- Across the studies, water ran from **41 to 59°F (5 to 15°C)** and immersions lasted **5 to 20 minutes**.
- The best results came from **52 to 59°F (11 to 15°C)**. The group at **50°F (10°C) or colder** did not show a significant benefit.
- The best time was **11 to 15 minutes**. The longest immersion tested, 16 to 20 minutes in one study at 41°F, did worse than resting.

The authors were careful about the size of the effect: cold water "can be slightly better" than passive recovery, and most of the included studies were of low quality.

A 2022 Cochrane review reached a similar overall conclusion. It included **17 small trials with 366 people** and found "some evidence" that cold water immersion reduces delayed-onset muscle soreness compared with rest, while rating the quality of the studies as low. It defined cold water immersion as water below **59°F (15°C)** and noted that the temperature, time and frequency varied widely.

## What the winter swimmers did

You may have seen "11 minutes a week" quoted as the ideal cold dose. It comes from a 2021 study by Søberg and colleagues in *Cell Reports Medicine* that compared young male winter swimmers with non-swimmers.

The swimmers averaged about **11 minutes a week in cold water** and **57 minutes a week in a sauna**, across about 2 to 3 swims a week. They had higher heat production in the cold than the comparison group.

That figure is a description of what a small group of 7 to 8 experienced swimmers happened to do, not a tested dose. The study cannot show that 11 minutes causes any benefit, and it included only men. The University of Copenhagen's own release notes the small sample and that no causal conclusions can be drawn.

## How much colder is "cold"?

A 2000 study by Šrámek and colleagues gives a sense of how strong the body's response gets as water cools. Young men sat in water up to the neck for **an hour** at three temperatures:

- At **68°F (20°C)**, metabolic rate rose by about **93%**.
- At **57°F (14°C)**, metabolic rate rose by about **350%**, and blood noradrenaline rose by about **530%**.

An hour at 57°F in a lab is far beyond a normal home plunge. The point is that you do not need near-freezing water to get a strong response. The 50s °F already produce it.

## A sensible way to start

The research above was done on healthy, supervised volunteers. For a home plunge, this is a cautious starting point (our suggestion, not a study protocol):

1. **Start around 55 to 60°F** for **1 to 3 minutes**.
2. **Breathe slowly** through the first moments. The initial gasp and fast breathing are the most dangerous part.
3. **Keep your head above water.**
4. **Add time before you lower the temperature.** The soreness research gives no reason to go below about 52°F.
5. **Get out** if you shiver hard, feel numb, lose coordination or feel unwell.

## Safety: who should not plunge

A 2017 review by Tipton and colleagues, titled "Cold water immersion: kill or cure?", describes cold water immersion as a known precursor to drowning, cardiac arrest and hypothermia, and says the evidence for many claimed benefits is varied and sometimes only anecdotal.

Practical rules that follow from that:

- **Never plunge alone**, and never after drinking alcohol.
- **Talk to a doctor first** if you have heart disease, high blood pressure or a heart rhythm problem. The 2018 Mayo Clinic Proceedings sauna review specifically says jumping into cold water right after a sauna is not usually recommended for people with heart disease.
- **Let a chiller or ice bath warm slightly** if you are new. Colder is not better for most goals.

## If you lift weights

Timing matters more than temperature here. A 2015 study found that regular cold plunges straight after strength training reduced gains in muscle size and strength. We cover that, and the other claimed benefits, in our guide to [cold plunge benefits](/cold-plunge-benefits/).
