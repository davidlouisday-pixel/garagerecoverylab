## The short answer

| | Infrared sauna | Red light therapy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | Heats your body until you sweat | Shines low-level red or near-infrared light on the skin |
| Feels | Hot, sweaty | Warm at most; not meant to heat you |
| Session | Usually 15 to 30 minutes in a cabin | Usually minutes, at a panel, mask or handheld device |
| Main evidence | Part of the wider sauna research; small studies | Mostly small skin studies; "not enough evidence" for most uses |
| Best for | Heat, relaxation, the sauna habit | Targeted skin goals |

## How they work

### Infrared sauna: heat

An infrared sauna uses infrared heaters to warm your body directly, usually at a lower air temperature than a traditional sauna. The point is the same as any sauna: raise your body temperature, get your heart rate up and sweat. We compare the two sauna types in [infrared vs traditional sauna](/infrared-vs-traditional-sauna/).

### Red light therapy: light, not heat

Red light therapy goes by other names, including **low-level laser therapy** and **photobiomodulation**. According to Cleveland Clinic, it uses **low levels of red light**, from lasers or LEDs, and the wavelength affects how deeply the light reaches into the skin. It is not designed to warm you up.

The two overlap in one way: both can involve near-infrared wavelengths. But a sauna uses that energy as heat for your whole body, while a red light device delivers a measured dose of light to a patch of skin.

## What the evidence says

### Infrared saunas

The strongest sauna evidence, such as the Finnish KIHD cohort, comes from **traditional** saunas. A 2018 systematic review of dry sauna studies by Hussain and Cohen concluded regular dry sauna bathing **has potential health benefits**, but most studies were small and few were randomized trials. We cover infrared-specific studies in [infrared sauna benefits](/infrared-sauna-benefits/).

### Red light therapy

Cleveland Clinic's summary is blunt: **there is not enough evidence to support most uses** of red light therapy. Some results show **promise for certain skin conditions**. Most studies are small, lack placebo groups or are done in animals. It lists weight loss, cellulite and depression among uses without evidence.

One of the better-known skin trials is Wunsch and Matuschka's 2014 controlled study in *Photomedicine and Laser Surgery*. **113 volunteers** were treated twice a week with red or broader red-and-near-infrared light and compared with **23 controls**. The treated groups showed improvements in **skin complexion, skin roughness and collagen density** measured by ultrasound, and blinded ratings of photos confirmed the difference. The broader spectrum showed **no advantage** over red light alone.

## Which one fits your goal?

| Your goal | Better fit |
| --- | --- |
| Sweat, heat and relaxation | Infrared (or traditional) sauna |
| A habit tied to the long-term sauna research | Traditional sauna |
| Fine lines or skin texture | Red light therapy, with modest expectations |
| Weight loss | Neither; sauna weight loss is water, see [sauna for weight loss](/sauna-for-weight-loss/) |
| Small space, low budget | A red light panel or mask takes far less room than a sauna |

## Safety

- **Red light:** Cleveland Clinic warns that misusing devices can damage your **skin or eyes** if they are not protected, and long-term safety is not known. Use the goggles that come with panels.
- **Infrared sauna:** the usual sauna rules apply. Drink water, skip alcohol, and ask a doctor first if you have heart disease, low blood pressure or are pregnant.

## Cole's take

Cole's routine is a traditional electric sauna with rocks at his gym, about **20 minutes at 180 to 190°F**, four or more times a week. He says it leaves him relaxed and loose. He has not used red light therapy or an infrared sauna, so this comparison relies on the research above.
