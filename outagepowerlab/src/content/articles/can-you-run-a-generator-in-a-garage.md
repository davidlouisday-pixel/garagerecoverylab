This is a safety guide built from U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidance, read on November 27, 2026. The answer does not depend on the brand, the size of the garage, or how wide the door is open.

## The short answer: never in a garage

The CDC is direct: "Never use a generator inside your home or garage, even if doors and windows are open."

The CPSC says the same, and lists the places that are not safe: never operate a portable generator "inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed or other enclosed spaces. Opening doors or windows will not provide enough ventilation to prevent the buildup of lethal levels of CO." It also warns against running one on a porch or in a carport.

That includes an attached garage with the big door up, a detached garage, and a shed.

## Why it kills people

Gasoline, propane and diesel engines make carbon monoxide (CO), a gas you cannot see or smell. In an enclosed or partly enclosed space it builds up faster than it clears, and it seeps from an attached garage into the house.

The numbers from the CPSC:

- An average of **nearly 100 people die each year** in the U.S. from CO poisoning from portable generators (2017 to 2019 data).
- Since 2009, portable generators were associated with an estimated **765 deaths**, about **40 percent** of all consumer product CO deaths under CPSC's jurisdiction.
- **More than half** of the deaths happen in the four cold months, November through February, when outages from winter storms push people to run generators close to home.
- In a CPSC review of deaths involving engine-driven tools, a CO alarm was present in only about **8 percent** of cases.

Generators sold in the U.S. carry a CPSC-required label that reads, in part: "Using a generator indoors can KILL YOU IN MINUTES."

## Where to put the generator instead

Follow both agencies' rules:

1. **Outside only.** Not in the garage, basement, shed, porch or carport.
2. **More than 20 feet away** from any windows, doors and vents (CDC). The CPSC says at least 20 feet from the home.
3. **Exhaust pointed away** from your house and your neighbors' (CPSC).
4. **Keep it dry.** Use a cover or canopy made for generators, not an enclosure.

Twenty feet is farther than most extension cords reach comfortably, which is one reason people cheat and set the generator by the garage door. Buy a heavy-duty cord long enough for the distance, rated for the load.

## Protect the house too

- **Install CO alarms** on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas (CPSC). Interconnected alarms are best, because when one sounds, they all sound.
- **Use battery-powered or battery-backup alarms**, because a plug-in alarm does nothing during an outage (CDC).
- **Test them monthly** and replace batteries at least once a year, or buy alarms with sealed 10-year batteries (CPSC).
- **If an alarm sounds, get outside immediately, then call 911** (CPSC).
- **Look for a CO shut-off generator.** The CPSC says models certified to PGMA G300-2018 and UL 2201 are estimated to reduce CO deaths by 87 percent and 100 percent.

## Do not plug the generator into the house

Running the generator outside solves the CO problem. Connecting it to your home's wiring creates a different one. Feeding a house through a wall or dryer outlet can send power back onto the utility lines, called backfeeding. HomeGuide notes that connecting a generator to a house without a transfer switch or interlock is illegal in many places because of the fire and electrocution risk. The only safe connections are extension cords to individual appliances, or a transfer switch or interlock kit installed by a licensed electrician. See [what a transfer switch costs](/generator-transfer-switch-cost/).

## Safer alternatives that can live in the garage

- **Battery power stations** produce no exhaust, so they are safe to use indoors. They are best for a fridge, lights, phones, Wi-Fi and a CPAP. Our [power station runtime calculator](/power-station-runtime-calculator/) shows how long one lasts.
- **A standby generator** sits permanently outside on a pad, installed to code clearances, and switches on by itself. See [what a whole-house generator costs](/how-much-does-a-whole-house-generator-cost/).
